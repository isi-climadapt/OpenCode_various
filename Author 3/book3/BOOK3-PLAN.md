## Why this story

Same engine as Books 1-2: a natural change the hero misreads as a crime, a calm friend who knows
the answer, a line-up of suspects with funny alibis, nature as the reveal, a closing gag, a facts
page.

The natural change is **a summer thunderstorm at night**. Banjo is about to do his biggest glide
ever when BOOM, his tree shakes. Every suspect is cleared by the storm itself: the sky flashes
while Cockatoo's wings are folded, and it booms while Lyrebird's beak is shut. The emotional theme
is being scared of storms, and the reveal hands kids a real tool: count from the flash to the boom,
and bigger numbers mean the storm is moving away. The scary part stays short and comic, lightning
is always far off in the sky (never touching a tree or an animal), and everyone ends up safe and dry
in Wombat's burrow.

## Art direction (changes from Book 2)

- Palette: storm night of deep indigo, storm violet and slate blue, with warm ochre and pink gum
  bark and rain-washed eucalyptus greens as the color accents. Lightning is a soft lilac-white glow
  inside the clouds, far away over the hills. After the storm: moonlight silver, puddles holding
  the moon. The last pages: pink and gold dawn.
- Rain as soft, slanted streaks and round splashy drops, never harsh.
- The storm is dramatic but friendly: no lightning bolts hitting trees or animals, no fire, no
  broken branches, no frightening faces in the clouds.
- Every image leaves an open area for text, using the Book 1-2 wording ("open sky, empty, for text,
  continuous with the scene, no hard edge"). On dark night skies the typesetter uses cream text or
  the cream panels Book 2 used on busy skies.
- Each character appears once per image. No text, letters or signs in any image (rule 2).

### Pages 30-31: Facts spread

Heading: **True Facts from the Bush**

- **Lightning** is a giant spark of electricity in a storm cloud. It heats the air so fast that the
  air bursts with a BOOM. That BOOM is thunder.
- **Flash, then boom.** Light travels much faster than sound, so you see lightning before you hear
  thunder. Count the seconds in between: every 5 seconds means the storm is about 1 mile away.
- **When thunder roars, go indoors.** The safest place to watch a storm is from inside.
- **Superb lyrebirds** copy the songs and calls of more than 20 kinds of birds, so well that it's
  hard to tell them apart. At night they sleep up in the trees.
- **Green tree frogs** call loudly after rain: "crawk, crawk, crawk."
- **The smell of rain** has a name: petrichor. Two Australian scientists named it in 1964.
- **Koalas** hug tree trunks on hot days, because the trunk is cooler than the air.
- **Wombats** dig long burrows under the ground, where they stay safe and dry.
- **Sugar gliders** sleep in tree hollows lined with leaves, often curled up together in a group.

Art: spot illustrations cropped from the story vignettes (p23 Banjo and Olive counting, p25's four

Facts checked 24 Oct 2026:
- Lyrebird: imitates over 20 bird species (Evolutionary Ecology, 2022); roosts in trees at night
  (Victoria Wildlife fact sheet, Superb Lyrebird). The popular "80% of its song is copied" figure
  isn't from a measured study, so it isn't used.
- Petrichor: coined by CSIRO's Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas, *Nature*, 7 March 1964 (CSIRO).
- Koalas: Briscoe et al. 2014, *Biology Letters*, "Tree-hugging koalas demonstrate a novel
  thermoregulatory mechanism for arboreal mammals" (trunks over 5 °C cooler than the air).
- Flash-to-bang: 5 seconds per mile is the standard approximation (sound speed varies with
  temperature). "When thunder roars, go indoors" is the US National Weather Service slogan.
- Green tree frogs: males call often after rain (NSW Field of Mars EEC fact sheet); the call is a
  deep repeated "crawk crawk" (Queensland Museum).
- Still to confirm at typesetting: sugar gliders' leaf-lined nests (standard; cite a state wildlife
  source), wombat burrows.
